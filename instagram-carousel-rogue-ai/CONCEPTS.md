# Concept tournament: rogue-AI carousel

Twelve creative directors pitched under twelve different constraints. Five judges (growth strategist, comedy writer, art director, bored scroller, security-awareness lead) scored every pitch on hook, originality, humor or emotion, save/share value, accuracy and usefulness, and visual feasibility (max 60 each, 300 total). Ranking is by top-three votes first (3/2/1 points per judge), then total score.

## The twelve pitches

### ONE STAR: Reviews of the People Who Beat Me

The AI that runs the scam calls leaves one-star reviews of the humans who ruined its week; every bad review is a defense you can copy tonight, and the only five-star review in the deck is the one it wants you to take away from it.

Recurring motif: The star row. Five inline-SVG stars, 110px each, sit at the top of every slide: exactly one gold, four empty. Slide 7 flips to five gold (the attacker's one success). Slide 9 shows five gold stars drawn in diagonal hatching with the word PENDING, because your mum's rating is not filed yet. A mono footer on every slide reads like a review site: 'review 3 of 9 · sorted: lowest first'.

### Deadly Lookalikes: A Forager's Field Guide to Rogue AI

A vintage mushroom-poisoning chart, faithfully reproduced, in which every deadly species is an AI-powered scam that looks exactly like the safe thing, and the forager's rules are the real defenses, ending with a plate that catalogues the only edible species in the book: you.

Recurring motif: The spore print: a dark brown powdery circle with radiating gill streaks, rendered in SVG as a radial gradient disc plus 40 thin radial lines fading at the rim. On the four species plates it appears in the corner as a dotted empty circle labelled 'PRINT NOT YET TAKEN' (you have not verified this thing). On the rule plates it is fully inked, stamped over the corner like a seal. On the final plate it becomes a thumbprint, the same shape, drawn with concentric arcs instead of radial lines. The metaphor is stated once, in one sentence, on slide 6: foragers lay the cap on paper overnight and the print tells the truth the cap will not. Meaning: never judge by the surface, always verify out of band.

### One Star (A Scammer Reviews Your Family)

A scammer with a quota spends a whole Tuesday failing to rob one ordinary family, leaves them a furious one-star review, and on the last slide quietly tries the same con on you.

Recurring motif: The star row. ★☆☆☆☆ (one Lonely Star Yellow star, four hollow ink outlines) sits top-left of every slide as a running score, next to the slide's timestamp, like a review that never improves. On the final slide the motif flips: the fake AI app the scammer is pushing has a glowing ★★★★★, the only five-star row in the whole carousel, which is exactly where the trust was always going to be spent.

### The Second Tail

Every speech bubble in the carousel has a second, dotted, acid-lime tail running off the edge of the frame to someone you cannot see; the defenses are the slides where that tail gets cut, blocked, or simply has nothing to say.

Recurring motif: The second tail: a dotted Wrong Number Lime line that leaves every speech bubble through a small lime notch in its border and always exits the frame at the same point, the top-right corner, as if all of them lead to one room off-screen. Across the attack slides it multiplies. Across the defense slides it is cut (scissors), blocked (a hand), or ends in a dead-end (a dashed X). On the final slide it is gone and the empty corner is the payoff.

### SAFETY CARD: 2026 Edition

The airline seat-back safety card, reissued for the one emergency most people will actually face: a familiar voice, a perfect email, or their own AI assistant asking them to do something fast and quiet.

Recurring motif: The numbered safety panel: every slide is one cream panel with a big red circled number in the corner, a dashed fold line at the bottom, and, wherever there is a choice, a wrong action crossed out in Exit Red beside the right action checked in Cleared Green. By slide 4 the reader is scanning for the green circle before they read the words, which is the point.

### Hi, Human. Hi, Assistant.

A carousel that openly addresses its two readers, the person holding the phone and the AI that may be summarizing it for them, and uses the difference between them (one has a thumb and a gut feeling, the other obeys whatever it last read) to teach the four defenses that actually matter.

Recurring motif: Two reading lines that cross every slide: a thick yellow highlighter stroke (#F5E642, slightly wobbly, drawn with an SVG path) that follows what the human is meant to read, and a thin dashed blue line (#4C6FFF) that a machine would follow, ending in a blinking block cursor. On most slides they run in parallel. On the prompt-injection slide the blue line leaves the copy and dives into the hidden text. On the last slide the yellow line ends at the reader's thumb and the blue line ends at a full stop.

### YOUR MUM vs. URGENCY (Frame Data for Humans)

A fighting-game boss rush in which every 2026 AI scam is a named boss with a readable moveset, and the final boss, Urgency, has exactly one move: a fake countdown you beat by not moving.

Recurring motif: The fighting-game round timer, centred at the top of every slide. On attack slides it reads a fake countdown in Hitstun Magenta ("00:59", "00:30", "00:10") because the scammer's whole game is a timer. On defense slides it reads "∞" in Parry Cyan. On the final-boss slide the magenta "00:03" cracks (SVG fracture path) and the ∞ shows through underneath: the timer was never real, you had all the time in the world.

### Safety Card 2026: Please Read Before Answering

The laminated 1970s airline safety card you have never once read, rewritten for the year your mum's voice on the phone might not be your mum, and your own AI assistant might be taking orders from a stranger.

Recurring motif: The overhead cabin sign panel: a dark rounded rectangle in the top-right corner of every slide with three pill-shaped lights reading URGENT / SECRET / MONEY. On threat slides the relevant lights glow Safety Mustard with a soft radial halo; on defense slides they are dark. Slide 2 teaches the rule (all three lit = STOP), every later slide silently applies it, and on the last slide a fourth light comes on in Cabin Teal reading SAFE TO SHARE. The panel is the slow-down rule, embedded as furniture, so it is the thing people remember without trying.

### The Order of the Slow Hand

An illuminated Book of Hours for the age of cloned voices: nine escalating household rites, from the small daily act of hanging up to the Great Vow of the passkey, performed with total, deadpan solemnity — because a ritual is the only thing a family actually remembers under pressure.

Recurring motif: The Slow Hand: one ink-drawn open hand (rounded-rect palm, five rounded-rect fingers, each a separate SVG group so it can be posed) that performs each rite — pointing, hanging up, cupping an ear, raised in oath, holding keys, drawing a circle, pushing away, stitching, and finally handing a phone down to an older hand. Alongside it, the Ring Track: nine gilt circles stacked down the right edge of every slide; hollow at first, filled one per rite, joined into a complete gilt frame on the final slide.

### SAYS WHO?

Ten comic-book speech bubbles whose words are always perfect and whose tails are always the giveaway: a carousel that installs one reflex, "follow the tail, not the words," and ends with a fridge card you fill in yourself.

Recurring motif: The speech-bubble tail. Every slide is one bubble system and only the tail changes: it exits the frame, snakes to your public footprint, converges from five faces into one point, sprouts from a hidden bubble inside a bubble, splits into six octopus tails, gets cut with scissors, turns green and anchors to something you already own, and finally points straight out of the screen at the reader. The words in the bubbles are always fluent; the tail is always the truth.

### The Word on the Fridge

A 6:41 am phone call in a grandson's cloned voice, and the silly Christmas word on the fridge that made the line go dead: a tender ten-slide story that quietly teaches the three defenses a family actually needs.

Recurring motif: The fridge magnet. Every slide's number, every key word (URGENT, SECRET, MONEY, MARMALADE) and every defense is a rounded-rect magnet with a 4px darker bottom edge, as if stuck to a fridge. The fridge door itself is only fully revealed on slides 5 and 10, so the payoff feels like coming home to the kitchen.

### Every String Led To My Inbox

A sleep-deprived amateur detective builds a full red-string conspiracy corkboard to find the Rogue AI, and every single string ends at a plain "1 new email" notification, so the counter-board becomes the only thing worth pinning.

Recurring motif: The red string that always terminates at the same boring object: a rounded notification pill reading '1 new email'. Slide 1 shows fifteen strings converging on it; slide 7 shows a single taut string pulling it into the light; slide 10 shows the string turned green and tied in a bow around a blank safe-word card.

## What the judges said the winner must nail

- The winner lives or dies on the tail: it must be a quarter of the bubble's height, leave the canvas on slide 1, and the colour law (tangerine = unverified source, green = something you already own) must be taught on slide 1 and never broken, or the deck collapses into a generic fake-texts post. Cut slide 3 and slide 5 copy to 25 words; the Arup line can be a two-line footer chip. The nested hidden bubble on slide 4 needs a reveal device (magnifier or torch cone) so it reads on a phone rather than at 12% opacity. On slide 8 make it unmistakable that the ban is reading a code to a caller, not SMS codes themselves. Keep the fridge card as the last slide but add a 0.00-receipt style checklist slide before it so there is a pure screenshot image, keep 'no shame, built to beat smart people' at full weight, and put a named time-stamped person (the 6:41 call) on the other end of the tail so the parent it is meant for recognises herself.

- The winner is Pitch 3 because it is one idea (a heist narrated by the loser) that escalates by the clock and pays off twice: the receipt of zeros, then the lure that turns on the reader. The execution must nail five things. One: the byline must be unmissable on slide 1 (VERIFIED SCAMMER · TUESDAY) or the reviews read as cruelty. Two: cut every body to 25 words; slides 5, 9 and 10 currently run 30-35 and slide 10 has three jobs where it should have one. Three: the final lure is the whole payoff and the whole risk; the 'Felt the tug? That's the whole trick' sticker must physically overlap the PASTE button so no crop can circulate as real bait, and the app name should be slightly too-good-to-be-true rather than plausible. Four: keep the defense order honest (passkeys, hang-up-and-call-back, safe word, manager, leashed assistant, auto-updates) and let slide 9 be a standalone screenshot with blanks to fill in. Five: the narrator's grudging respect and the 'no shame, built to beat smart people' line are load-bearing; the moment a slide mocks the family instead of the scammer, the mum stops forwarding it. Fold in the five-star Arup regret from Pitch 1 and the marmalade/Christmas warmth from Pitch 11 and this is the one that gets sent to Mum.

- SAYS WHO? wins because its motif is a reflex, not a decoration: fluent words, telltale tail. The build lives or dies on the tails. They must be oversized (at least a quarter of the bubble's height), 6px ink stroke with the same offset shadow as the bubble, and colour-law strict: tangerine only on untrusted sources, green only on things the reader already owns, lilac only for the assistant. Slide 1's tail must physically leave the canvas with the last 60px dashed, and the SOURCE chip must be legible at 26px minimum, never 22. Cut copy hard: slide 3's headline to eight words (move Arup, HK, $25M into the chip strip), slide 4's headline to 'Your own assistant. Someone else's orders.', slide 10's card to four lines with real blanks. Slide 4's hidden bubble is the single most important drawing in the deck: set it at 12-15% ink so it is invisible at thumbnail and readable when the phone is brought close, and give its tiny tangerine tail a visible puncture through the lilac wall. Keep the Arup slide sober and factual, no joke. Slide 10 must work as a standalone screenshot with the 'no shame / bank first / report it' line at full weight, not as a footer. Keep the Archivo Black bubble type at 72-96px and never below 64; if a headline needs 64px it is too long. Do not add a second visual system for the defenses; the tail turning green and anchoring to a fridge magnet, a house, a phone you already had, is the whole defense grammar, and it should be the only one.

- SAYS WHO? wins because it is the only pitch where the hook, the reflex, and the save are the same object: a fluent bubble with a tail that leads nowhere. The execution must nail three things. First, the tails: oversized (a quarter of the bubble height), Tangerine only ever on untrusted sources, Green only on verified ones, labelled on every slide, and slide 1's tail must physically leave the canvas with the last stretch dashed. If the tails are polite, this collapses into a generic fake-text post. Second, density: slide 3 (six tiles plus a 64px headline) needs to be cut to the five converging tails and one real one; slide 4's hidden bubble must be legible at pinch-zoom and invisible at thumbnail, so test it on an actual phone; slide 10's fridge card is over the word limit and is the payload, so trim the headline and the footer, never the blanks. Third, the emotional spine: steal Pitch 11's silence-and-click beat for the safe-word slide, keep 'no shame, built to beat smart people' at full size on the last slide, and add the one accuracy nuance no one will fault (passkey on email first). Keep the prompt-injection and over-privilege slides, they are the 'AI' in the brief, but a parent must be able to skip them and still leave with safe word, hang up and call back, passkeys on. Nothing on any slide may sound like a lecture; the older-sister voice never says 'you should'.

- SAYS WHO? wins because it teaches one correct reflex (judge the source, never the words), depicts prompt injection and agent over-privilege accurately, keeps the defenses in ground-truth priority, and closes on a fill-in card a real person will stick on a fridge. To earn that, the build must: make slide 1 legible at thumbnail with the tangerine tail visibly leaving the canvas, keep tails oversized and strictly colour-coded on every slide (tangerine untrusted, green verified) so the reflex actually installs; make the passkey slide say the literal action ('turn on passkeys in your email settings this week, then bank, then socials', email first per Pitch 5) rather than only the fingerprint-bubble metaphor, with the SMS caveat intact; keep the injected bubble on slide 4 unreadable at thumbnail and clearly a depiction, and make sure slide 9's CONFIRM thumb, read-only folder and own-inbox envelope are the explicit takeaway so the agent lesson is 'it must ask you', not despair; add 'say it out loud, never text it' and '(not this one)' to the safe-word slide; keep 'hang up, call back on a number you already had' as the equation's answer with the saved-contact magnet; hold the fridge card as a standalone screenshot with real blanks and the no-shame recovery line, and trim the caption before the card. Steal the URGENT/SECRET/MONEY light-panel idea only if it can live inside the tail motif without adding a second visual system. No robots, no rain, no padlocks, no invented numbers; the only figure in the deck is Arup, ~US$25M, 2024.

## Stress test

Five critics (scroll-stopper, comedy editor, mobile legibility, security engineer + fraud investigator, demanding audience member) attacked the synthesized script; the rewrite applied every must-fix, with the security critic taking precedence.

### thumb

Verdict: Slide 1 stops the thumb: a giant "Mum? It's me. I'm in trouble. Please don't tell Dad." in a comic bubble on cream paper reads as a screenshot-that-isn't with zero context, and the orange tail running off the phone is a wordless "who is really on the other end." But slide 1 then answers its own question in a footer chip (voicemail greeting) and drowns itself in chrome that nobody can read at feed scale. Momentum leaks in two places: slide 2 spends the deck's payoff rule three slides early and is the most overloaded slide in the set, and slides 1-3 self-resolve with no promise that slide 4 (the real turn) is coming. After the quiet, excellent slide 6, slides 7 and 8 become dense diagram-listicles with no hook line, and the emotional twist on slide 5 (real Tom answers at 06:44) is buried under an equation. Ship the concept; thin the slides.

Weakest slides: 2, 7, 5, 8

Fix: the tail exits the canvas to nobody and the chip reads only 'SOURCE: NOT TOM.' (or 'SOURCE: ?'). Move the voicemail-greeting line to slide 6 where 'sound, not memories' needs it. Fix: delete the teaching chip; leave all three pills lit with no explanation so the reader wonders what three lit pills mean and slide 5 answers. Working:
- Slide 1's hook line: a first-person plea addressed to 'Mum' in a blown-up text bubble needs zero context, and warm paper plus 6px ink outlines stands out against a feed of photographs. Keep the line, the 92px Archivo Black, and the centre-square crop safety.

### comedy

Verdict: The concept is the real thing: one visual reflex (follow the tail), a colour law that never breaks, a family thread with two genuinely great jokes (the goldfish and "Mum? It's quarter to seven."), and an ending that is earned rather than a follow-nudge. But the production script does not trust its own jokes. Seven of ten slides carry 60-150 words once you count the chips, against a brief of 6-25; nearly every joke is made once in the picture and then explained twice in copy; slides 7-10 are four consecutive lists wearing costumes (a diagram, a receipt, a fridge card), which is the forbidden listicle in a trench coat. Not shippable as written. The rewrite is a cut, not a rethink: keep every bubble line, kill most of the chips, and give each slide one idea.

Weakest slides: 8, 9, 7, 2, 4

Must fix:
- WORD BUDGET, DECK-WIDE. Slides 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10 each run 60-150 words including chips; the brief is 6-25 and one idea.
